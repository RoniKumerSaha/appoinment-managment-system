The starting point

I had an idea. I didn't have a product.

"Build me an appointment system for clinics." That was the entire brief in my head. Clinics have multiple branches. Patients don't want accounts. Doctors work across branches. Receptionists need branch-scoped views. The list was half in my head, half on sticky notes.

What I knew

  • Multi-branch clinics exist
  • Patients don't want to sign up for one appointment
  • Double booking is the #1 thing to avoid
  • Receptionists work per-branch, not per-doctor

What I didn't know

  • Whether doctors should belong to one clinic or many
  • Whether SMS OTP was needed or overkill
  • What a "recurring appointment" actually meant here
  • How schedule edits should interact with existing bookings
The real problem

I didn't have a specification problem. I had an ambiguity problem. Every screen I imagined depended on rules I hadn't written down — and the rules depended on choices I hadn't made. Classic chicken-and-egg.

05 / 13
Stage 1 · Brainstorming

Mary didn't let me skip the hard part.

The brainstorming stage used several creative techniques to surface assumptions. The most useful one for me was forcing myself to name the contradictions before writing a single requirement.

What Mary asked me

  • "What does 'guest patient' actually mean here — no account, no record, or just no password?"
  • "If a doctor works at two branches, are those the same schedule or different schedules?"
  • "When a doctor blocks a day that has existing appointments, what happens?"
  • "Is recurring a patient feature or a staff feature?"

What got resolved

  • Guest patient = phone-number identity, OTP to view
  • Each branch has its own doctor schedule (branch-specific availability)
  • Schedule changes that break appointments are rejected; admin hours changes flag for manual fix
  • Recurring is staff-only — patients book one slot at a time
Pattern

When you can't decide, the AI asks you to name the question out loud. Once it's named, it stops being a background worry and becomes a line item you can solve. That's half of brainstorming's value.

Why this mattered

Once a rule is written and numbered, every later artifact — flow, screen, copy — has to be consistent with it. If a mockup violates BR-009, the mockup is wrong, not the rule.

07 / 13
How ambiguity disappeared

Each stage killed a specific kind of ambiguity.

I think of it as three layers of fog lifting in order.

Stage 1 · Vagueness

  • "It should work for clinics"
  • "Patients should be able to manage appointments"
  • "Doctors and receptionists have different views"

After Stage 1

  • Multi-branch, single clinic per doctor
  • OTP-verified phone identity, cancel + reschedule + view only
  • Receptionists are branch-scoped; doctors are clinic-scoped with branch assignments

Stage 2 · Undefined behavior

  • "What if two people book the same slot?"
  • "What if the doctor cancels a day?"
  • "Can a patient have two appointments on the same day?"

After Stage 3 (PRD)

  • BR-023 + BR-024: atomic slot reservation, fail fast on race
  • BR-009 + BR-055: schedule change rejected if it breaks appointments
  • BR-033: patient cannot have overlapping appointments — across any doctor

Working with the agents

How I actually interacted with the AI.

Most of the value came from a small number of habits. None of them are clever — they're just consistent.

What worked

  • One stage at a time. I never asked Sally about mockups while the PRD was still open.
  • Read the contradictions out loud. "What does this rule mean when X happens?" — name the edge case before answering.
  • Give examples, not adjectives. "Like Calendly" beats "modern and clean" every time.
  • Stick to one pattern per stage. Once Stacked Vertical Flow won, I used it as the default and only deviated with a justification.
  • Number the rules. BR-009 in the PRD matches BR-009 in the chat with the agent. That's how consistency is preserved.

What didn't work

  • Skipping stages. Asking Sally to mockup before the brief existed produced pretty, wrong screens.
  • Editing the AI's output mid-stage. I'd rewrite a rule, then the next agent contradicted it. Fixing the source is faster.
  • Vague reviews. "Make it pop" without a token reference produced drift from the design system.
  • Skipping out-of-scope. The "not in v1" list saved me from building login flows for patients — twice.
13 / 13
Takeaways

Three things I'd tell anyone doing this.

If I had to compress everything above into the smallest possible advice list, it would be these three.

1. Documents are checkpoints, not artifacts.

The point of the BRD and PRD isn't the document. It's the moment you have to commit a number to a rule. That moment is what removes ambiguity — not the file itself.

2. The agent's job is to ask, not to guess.

The best outputs came when I treated the agent like a structured interviewer. When I let it guess, it produced something plausible and wrong. When I forced it to ask, the gaps surfaced early.

3. Consistency beats coverage.

22 of 31 mockups using the same Stacked Vertical Flow pattern shipped cleaner than 31 unique patterns. One default, deviated from deliberately, is faster to build and easier to review.

What I'd try differently next time

  • Run bmad-brainstorming earlier — before I "knew" what the product was
  • Write the out-of-scope list before the in-scope list
  • Lock the design system before opening the first mockup file
"Ambiguity isn't solved by more thinking. It's solved by more writing." — what I learned on this project.
